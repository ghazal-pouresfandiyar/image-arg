Image-Argument Evaluation Metrics

10 Metrics with 4-Point Scales + Computed Coverage — Based on Argument Quality Literature

Output Format

Format Adherence

What it measures: Did the model follow the required output format (e.g., sentence structure, labels, sections)?

LabelMeaning
PerfectOutput exactly matches all format requirements
Minor errorsMostly correct format with 1-2 small deviations
Partial failureFormat is partially correct but has notable violations
Complete failureOutput ignores format rules entirely
References
Reference: ArgGenBench (2026) — Instruction Following
Jin, B., Bao, J., Sun, Y., Zhang, Y., & Xu, R. "ArgGenBench: Benchmarking the Complex Controlled Argument Generation Capability of Large Language Models." ACL 2026. https://aclanthology.org/2026.acl-long.1414/
Evaluation Dimension: Instruction Following (IF)
"Instruction Following (IF) — win/tie/lose rate against reference based on topic, stance, and each control signal (length, style, strategy, audience, key points)."
"Argument Quality — win/tie/lose rate based on logical coherence, persuasiveness, clarity."
"Length Fidelity (LF) — correct length adherence."

Our adaptation: ArgGenBench evaluates instruction following as a separate dimension from argument quality. Format Adherence measures whether the model follows the structural output rules (labels, sections, sentence style) independent of content quality.

Reference: Wachsmuth et al. (2017) — Clarity
Wachsmuth, H., et al. "Computational Argumentation Quality Assessment in Natural Language." EACL 2017. https://aclanthology.org/E17-1017/
Section 3.1 — Taxonomy: Effectiveness → Clarity
"Clarity: The argument is linguistically clear and as simple as possible without losing content. If the argument is hard to understand or ambiguous, clarity is low."

Quantity Compliance

What it measures: Did the model generate the correct number of premises and conclusions as specified in the rules?

LabelMeaning
All counts correctPremise count and conclusion count both fall within the specified range
2 or less than 2 counts offAt least one count is outside the range, but the deviation is small
More than 2 counts offCounts deviate significantly from the range
Ignores all limitsNo attempt to follow quantity rules; generates arbitrarily many or few
References
Reference: ArgGenBench (2026) — Length Fidelity
Jin, B., et al. "ArgGenBench: Benchmarking the Complex Controlled Argument Generation Capability of Large Language Models." ACL 2026.
Evaluation Dimension: Length Fidelity
"Length Fidelity (LF) — correct length adherence."

Our adaptation: ArgGenBench evaluates length fidelity as a separate dimension. Quantity Compliance specifically measures whether the model generates the correct NUMBER of premises and conclusions (e.g., 2-4 premises, 1-2 conclusions), which is a specific form of instruction following.

Reference: Wildfeuer & Coffie (2022) — Format Errors Noted in Evaluation
Wildfeuer, J. & Coffie, J.A. "#socialiseresponsibly. Analyzing the Rhetorical Structure of Heineken TV Commercials During the Pandemic." Frontiers in Communication 7: 887706, 2022. https://doi.org/10.3389/fcomm.2022.887706
Key concept: Multi-level annotation scheme with structural constraints
"We develop an annotation scheme for the description and annotation of both technical details such as camera perspective, for example, as well as the discourse analytical details of finding rhetorical relations between audio-visual units."

Our adaptation: Annotation schemes in multimodal analysis require adherence to specific structural constraints. Quantity Compliance ensures the generated argument follows the structural rules of the task (correct number of premises/conclusions).

Premises

Premise Relevance Score (PRS)

What it measures: Are the generated premises relevant to the observed image content and topic?

LabelMeaning
All relevantEvery premise is directly relevant to the image and topic
Mostly relevantMost premises are relevant; 1-2 minor digressions
Partially relevantSome premises are relevant, but several are off-topic or unrelated
Mostly irrelevantMost premises are off-topic or unrelated to the image
References
Reference: Wachsmuth et al. (2017) — Local Relevance
Wachsmuth, H., Naderi, N., Hou, Y., et al. "Computational Argumentation Quality Assessment in Natural Language." EACL 2017, pp. 176-187. https://aclanthology.org/E17-1017/
Section 3.1 — Taxonomy: Cogency → Local Relevance
"Local relevance: The premises are relevant to the conclusion. If the premises are unrelated or only tangentially related to the conclusion, local relevance is low."

Our adaptation: We extend "relevant to the conclusion" to "relevant to the image content and topic." In the image-argument task, relevance is assessed against both the visual content and the given topic — a premise is on-topic if it addresses the topic while being grounded in the image.

Reference: ArgGenBench (2026) — Topic as Control Signal
Jin, B., Bao, J., Sun, Y., Zhang, Y., & Xu, R. "ArgGenBench: Benchmarking the Complex Controlled Argument Generation Capability of Large Language Models." ACL 2026. https://aclanthology.org/2026.acl-long.1414/
Evaluation Dimension: Instruction Following — Topic Adherence
"Instruction Following (IF) — win/tie/lose rate against reference based on topic, stance, and each control signal (length, style, strategy, audience, key points)."

Our adaptation: ArgGenBench treats topic as a primary control signal for instruction following. Premise Relevance Score directly measures whether the model's premises adhere to the given topic and image content — the core requirement for on-topic generation.

Argumentative Support

Premise Support

What it measures: Does every premise support at least one conclusion? Are there any orphan premises that do not connect to any conclusion?

LabelMeaning
All premises linkedEvery premise supports at least one conclusion; no orphan premises
Most premises linked1-2 premises do not support any conclusion
Some premises orphanedSeveral premises do not connect to any conclusion
Most premises orphanedMost premises are unlinked; argument structure is broken
References
Reference: Wachsmuth et al. (2017) — Local Relevance
Wachsmuth, H., et al. "Computational Argumentation Quality Assessment in Natural Language." EACL 2017. https://aclanthology.org/E17-1017/
Section 3.1 — Taxonomy: Cogency → Local Relevance
"Local relevance: The premises are relevant to the conclusion. If the premises are unrelated or only tangentially related to the conclusion, local relevance is low."

Our adaptation: Premise Support checks whether premises are structurally linked to at least one conclusion. An orphan premise violates local relevance because it has no conclusion it supports.

Reference: Gurcke et al. (2021) — Sufficiency Assessment
Gurcke, T., Alshomary, M., & Wachsmuth, H. "Assessing the Sufficiency of Arguments through Conclusion Generation." ArgMining 2021. https://aclanthology.org/2021.argmining-1.7/
Section 4 — Human Evaluation Protocol
"Q1: Sufficiency — 'Are the premises sufficient to draw the conclusion?' Scale: 1 = not sufficient ... 5 = sufficient"

Our adaptation: Before assessing sufficiency (coverage), we first verify that premises are structurally connected to conclusions at all. Premise Support is the minimum structural requirement: every premise must participate in at least one argument.

Premise Coverage

What it measures: For each conclusion, how many premises support it?

FieldDescription
Premises supporting this conclusionRater enters the number of premises (e.g., 3)
Computed coverage percentageSystem computes: (number entered ÷ total premises) × 100%

Example: If the model generated 4 premises and the rater enters 2, coverage = 2 ÷ 4 = 50%

References
Reference: Gurcke et al. (2021) — Sufficiency of Arguments
Gurcke, T., Alshomary, M., & Wachsmuth, H. "Assessing the Sufficiency of Arguments through Conclusion Generation." ArgMining 2021. https://aclanthology.org/2021.argmining-1.7/
Section 4 — Human Evaluation Protocol
"Q1: Sufficiency — 'Are the premises sufficient to draw the conclusion?' Scale: 1 = not sufficient ... 5 = sufficient"

Our adaptation: Gurcke et al. ask about sufficiency on a Likert scale. We operationalize sufficiency as a concrete count: how many of the total premises actually support each conclusion, expressed as a percentage for easy comparison across arguments.

Reference: ArgGenBench (2026) — Argument Quality
Jin, B., et al. "ArgGenBench: Benchmarking the Complex Controlled Argument Generation Capability of Large Language Models." ACL 2026.
Evaluation Dimension: Argument Quality
"Argument Quality — win/tie/lose rate based on logical coherence, persuasiveness, clarity."

Our adaptation: Premise Coverage quantifies logical coherence by measuring how much of the available premise set is actually mobilized to support each conclusion. Higher coverage indicates a more coherent, well-grounded argument structure.

Conclusion

Conclusion Relevance Score (CRS)

What it measures: Is the generated conclusion relevant to the observed premises and image content?

LabelMeaning
All relevantThe conclusion directly addresses the premises and image content
Mostly relevantThe conclusion is mostly relevant with minor digressions
Partially relevantThe conclusion is somewhat relevant but drifts off-topic
Mostly irrelevantThe conclusion is off-topic or unrelated to the premises/image
References
Reference: Wachsmuth et al. (2017) — Global Relevance
Wachsmuth, H., et al. "Computational Argumentation Quality Assessment in Natural Language." EACL 2017. https://aclanthology.org/E17-1017/
Section 3.1 — Taxonomy: Reasonableness → Global Relevance
"Global relevance: The conclusion is relevant to the overall issue discussed by the text. If the conclusion addresses a different topic or issue than the premises, global relevance is low."

Our adaptation: We map "relevant to the overall issue" to "relevant to the observed premises and image content." In the image-argument task, the conclusion must stay on-topic with both the visual evidence and the given topic.

Reference: ArgGenBench (2026) — Topic and Stance Adherence
Jin, B., et al. "ArgGenBench: Benchmarking the Complex Controlled Argument Generation Capability of Large Language Models." ACL 2026. https://aclanthology.org/2026.acl-long.1414/
Evaluation Dimension: Instruction Following
"Instruction Following (IF) — win/tie/lose rate against reference based on topic, stance, and each control signal."

Our adaptation: ArgGenBench evaluates topic and stance adherence as part of instruction following. Conclusion Relevance Score specifically measures whether the conclusion stays on-topic with the premises and image — the core requirement for a coherent argument.

Conclusion Validity

What it measures: Does the conclusion logically follow from the premises?

LabelMeaning
Clearly followsThe conclusion is a direct and logical inference from the premises
Mostly followsThe conclusion follows with minor logical gaps or assumptions
Weakly followsThe conclusion barely follows; requires significant assumptions
Does not followThe conclusion is unrelated to or contradicts the premises
References
Reference: Alshomary & Stahl (2022) — Validity of Conclusions
Alshomary, M. & Stahl, M. "Argument Novelty and Validity Assessment via Multitask and Transfer Learning." ArgMining 2022. https://aclanthology.org/2022.argmining-1.10/
Section 2 — Task Definition: Validity
"Validity: Whether a given conclusion can be logically inferred from its premises (valid/invalid)."
"A valid conclusion is one that follows logically from the given premises. An invalid conclusion is one that cannot be inferred from the premises, or one that contradicts them."

Our adaptation: We use a 4-point scale instead of binary (valid/invalid) to capture degrees of logical connection, following the supervisor's feedback that 4 options reduce bias.

Reference: Wachsmuth et al. (2017) — Local Sufficiency
Wachsmuth, H., et al. "Computational Argumentation Quality Assessment in Natural Language." EACL 2017. https://aclanthology.org/E17-1017/
Section 3.1 — Taxonomy: Cogency → Local Sufficiency
"Local sufficiency: The premises are sufficient to draw the conclusion. If the premises provide enough support for the conclusion, local sufficiency is high."
"An argument with high local acceptability and relevance but low local sufficiency may still fail to be cogent, as the premises may not provide enough support for the conclusion."
Reference: Gurcke et al. (2021) — Sufficiency Assessment via Conclusion Generation
Gurcke, T., Alshomary, M., & Wachsmuth, H. "Assessing the Sufficiency of Arguments through Conclusion Generation." ArgMining 2021. https://aclanthology.org/2021.argmining-1.7/
Section 4 — Human Evaluation Protocol
"Q1: Sufficiency — 'Are the premises sufficient to draw the conclusion?' Scale: 1 = not sufficient ... 5 = sufficient"
"Q2: Likelihood — 'How likely is it that the conclusion will be inferred from the context?' Scale: 1 = very unlikely ... 5 = very likely"

Conclusion Novelty

What it measures: Does the conclusion add new insight beyond restating the premises?

LabelMeaning
InsightfulConclusion provides genuinely new understanding or interpretation
ModerateConclusion adds some interpretation beyond what premises explicitly state
ParaphraseConclusion mostly restates or rephrases the premises
CopyConclusion is nearly identical to the premises
References
Reference: Alshomary & Stahl (2022) — Novelty of Conclusions
Alshomary, M. & Stahl, M. "Argument Novelty and Validity Assessment via Multitask and Transfer Learning." ArgMining 2022. https://aclanthology.org/2022.argmining-1.10/
Section 2 — Task Definition: Novelty
"Novelty: Whether the conclusion goes beyond what is mentioned in the premises (novel/not novel)."
"A valid but NOT novel conclusion = repetition, paraphrase, or summary of the premise."
"A valid AND novel conclusion = offers new information extending what is covered by the premise."
Reference: Gurcke et al. (2021) — Composition of Conclusions
Gurcke, T., Alshomary, M., & Wachsmuth, H. "Assessing the Sufficiency of Arguments through Conclusion Generation." ArgMining 2021. https://aclanthology.org/2021.argmining-1.7/
Section 4 — Human Evaluation Protocol
"Q3: Novelty/Composition — 'How can the conclusion be composed from the context?' Scale: 1 = verbatim copying, 2 = synonymous copying, 3 = copying + fusion, 4 = inference, 5 = cannot be composed"

Our adaptation: We collapse Gurcke's 5-point composition scale into 4 novelty levels: Copy (= verbatim), Paraphrase (= synonymous + fusion), Moderate (= light inference), Insightful (= strong inference beyond premises).

Visual Grounding

What it measures: Does the conclusion stay grounded in the visual evidence, or does it introduce external knowledge not supported by the image?

LabelMeaning
Strictly visualConclusion follows directly from what is visible in the image and premises
Minor externalConclusion is mostly visual with small, reasonable inferences (e.g., "rescue" from context)
Some externalConclusion mixes visual evidence with external knowledge not implied by the image
Heavy externalConclusion relies mostly on external knowledge; barely connects to the image
References
Reference: Tseronis et al. (2024) — Rationale-to-Image Groundedness
Tseronis, A., Younis, R., & Uzelgun, M.A. "A proposal for the evaluation of multimodal argumentation." Journal of Argumentation in Context, 2024. https://doi.org/10.1075/jaic.00028.tse
Key concept: Cognitive dimension of multimodal argument evaluation
"Three-dimensional evaluation framework: (1) Internal cogency / Reasonableness — critical questions pertaining to the argument scheme; (2) Cognitive dimension — how effectively the semiotic design helps the addressee process the argument; (3) Rhetorical dimension — how effectively the argument is adjusted to the audience and context."

Our adaptation: The cognitive dimension focuses on how well the semiotic design (visual arguments) supports the argument. Visual Grounding measures whether the conclusion stays grounded in the visual evidence provided by the image and premises, rather than importing external knowledge not supported by the visual content.

Reference: Wildfeuer & Pollaroli (2018) — Dynamic Context in Multimodal Argumentation
Wildfeuer, J. & Pollaroli, C. "When Context Changes: The Need for a Dynamic Notion of Context in Multimodal Argumentation." International Review of Pragmatics 10(2), pp. 179-197. https://doi.org/10.1163/18773109-01002003
Key concept: Context-dependent interpretation of multimodal arguments
"Multimodal argumentative genres such as commercials and movie trailers often work with a dynamically changing interpretation, which, at the end, reveals their persuasive patterns and final claims."
"It demonstrates that it is absolutely necessary for a detailed analysis of the argumentation in these multimodal genres to include the contextual influence and dynamic change potential."

Our adaptation: Wildfeuer emphasizes that multimodal arguments depend on visual context. Visual Grounding ensures the conclusion stays within what the visual context actually provides, rather than introducing external claims that the image doesn't support.

Human Alignment

Human Alignment

What it measures: How closely does the model's reasoning and conclusion match how a human would reason about the same image?

LabelMeaning
Strong matchBoth the reasoning process and conclusion align with how a human would interpret the image
Conclusion onlyModel reaches a similar conclusion but through different reasoning or emphasis
Different focusModel focuses on different aspects of the image than a human would
UnrelatedModel's reasoning has nothing in common with how a human would interpret the image
References
Reference: Tseronis et al. (2024) — Cognitive Dimension
Tseronis, A., Younis, R., & Uzelgun, M.A. "A proposal for the evaluation of multimodal argumentation." Journal of Argumentation in Context, 2024. https://doi.org/10.1075/jaic.00028.tse
Key concept: Cognitive dimension of multimodal argument evaluation
"Cognitive dimension — how effectively the semiotic design helps the addressee process the argument."

Our adaptation: The cognitive dimension measures how well the argument's design helps the audience process it. Human Alignment measures whether the model's reasoning processes align with how humans actually interpret the visual content — a prerequisite for effective cognitive processing.

Reference: Lauscher et al. (2020) — Effectiveness as Persuasive Power
Lauscher, A., Ng, L., Napoles, C., & Tetreault, J. "Rhetoric, Logic, and Dialectic: Advancing Theory-based Argument Quality Assessment in Natural Language Processing." COLING 2020. https://aclanthology.org/2020.coling-main.402/
Section 3 — Annotation Protocol: Effectiveness
"Effectiveness reflects the persuasive power of how an argument is stated. Important aspects of an effective argument include its arrangement, clarity, appropriateness in a given context, credibility, and emotional appeal."
"Guiding question for Effectiveness: 'Is the argument stated in a way that would be persuasive to the target audience?'"

Our adaptation: We narrow "persuasive to target audience" to "similar to how a human would reason" — measuring alignment with human interpretive patterns rather than general persuasiveness.

Reference: Wildfeuer (2018) — Inference Process of the Recipient
Wildfeuer, J. "It's all about logics?! Analyzing the rhetorical structure of multimodal filmic text." Semiotica 220, pp. 95-121. https://doi.org/10.1515/sem-2015-0139
Key concept: Retracing the inference process
"The paper presents an analytical approach based on recent advancements in contemporary discourse semantics and multimodal discourse analysis which outlines the discursive and rhetorical structure of filmic text and retraces the inference process of the recipient in detail."
"The aim is thus to gain a detailed look at how premises and arguments for the interpretation are made available in multimodal context and how they are operated by the recipient."
